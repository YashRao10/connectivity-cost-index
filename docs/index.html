The question

Satellite broadband carries a well-known reputation for being expensive relative to its speed — a "tax" paid for coverage in places wired options can't reach. This project tests that claim with public data instead of assuming it: FCC's National Broadband Map (which technologies actually serve which locations, split by GSO vs. LEO satellite) joined against published ISP/carrier pricing, across 10 states chosen for regional spread — West Coast (CA, WA), Sun Belt (TX, FL), Midwest (OH), Mountain West (CO, MT), and Northeast/major-metro (NJ, NY, NC).

The finding complicates the premise, and holds up as the sample grows. LEO satellite (Starlink) is not the worst value in any of the 10 states — it beats legacy GSO satellite, DSL, and every flavor of fixed wireless everywhere, and only loses to cable and fiber where those are actually available. The sharpest result: Starlink beats Licensed Fixed Wireless (Verizon 5G Home / T-Mobile Home Internet — the actual 5G-home-internet product, not a niche one) by exactly 10× on cost-per-Mbps, $0.196 vs. $2.00, in every single one of the 10 states — dense metros (NJ, NY, CA) and rural states (MT) alike. This was true at 3 states; it's just as true at 10 across every region type tested. The real story is less "satellite is a tax" and more "satellite beats most of its terrestrial wireless competition outright, and loses only to wired infrastructure where it exists."

Worth flagging up front, not buried in methodology: Starlink and Licensed Fixed Wireless show zero cross-state variance in this dataset ($0.196 and $2.00 exactly everywhere) because both use one national price point against a technology-level FCC median that doesn't move much by state. Fiber, Cable, GSO Satellite, and DSL vary meaningfully by state because their real per-state median speeds do. The 10x gap is real and the underlying market structure (one national LEO provider, one dominant national price) is itself part of the finding — but it's a mechanical constant, not 10 independent confirmations of the same result. See methodology for the full caveat.

Real-world speed vs. advertised (Ookla)

FCC's numbers above are provider-reported advertised speeds. This section checks them against what people actually measure: median blended speed/latency per state from Ookla's public Speedtest data (Q2 2026), across every technology mix in that state — not filtered to one technology.

These are blended medians across every technology serving a state — not "fiber's real speed" or "satellite's real speed" specifically. A high-fiber-density state's blended fixed median sitting far below fiber's advertised "up to 8000 Mbps" isn't fiber underperforming; it's that the real-world mix, including slower DSL/wireless/satellite lines, pulls the state-wide median down. Mobile is meaningfully slower than fixed everywhere. Across the 10 states, fixed median download ranges from 171.1 Mbps (MT, rural) to 398.8 Mbps (FL) — MT also has the highest mobile latency (49ms) and lowest mobile test volume by far, consistent with a thinner cellular buildout. This layer now uses a real Census-shapefile point-in-polygon join per state (upgraded from an earlier bounding-box approximation).

Where the subsidy money goes: BEAD allocation vs. satellite dependence

The numbers below are pre-restructuring and known to be significantly out of date. They reflect BEAD's original June 2023 state allocations. In 2025, NTIA restructured the program ("Benefit of the Bargain"), and actual approved/provisional awards have come in far lower for at least some states verified so far: New Jersey's final approved award is ~$62M against a $263.7M original allocation (a 76% cut); Texas's provisional award is ~$1.27B against $3.31B (~62% cut); Colorado's is ~$397M against $826.5M (~52% cut). This is not a rounding difference — it's a materially different program than the one these figures describe. Also notable: California's approved plan reports its BEAD-funded locations split 52.9% fiber / 27% LEO satellite / 20.1% fixed wireless, a directly relevant data point for this project that isn't reflected in the simple allocation-total figure below at all. Full re-pull against verified final/provisional awards for every state is the next planned step for this layer — treat everything below as illustrative of the original 2023 formula's logic, not current reality.

The federal BEAD program allocated $42.45B across states in 2023 to build broadband infrastructure. Does that money track where the market gaps actually are? As a first-pass proxy, GSO Satellite locations served (from the FCC layer above) stands in for "underserved" locations — BEAD's own eligibility rules treat satellite as non-qualifying/lower-tier service in most cases, so places with heavy GSO satellite coverage plausibly correlate with places lacking adequate terrestrial options.

The spread is wide: Montana gets ~$444 per GSO-satellite-served location vs. New Jersey's ~$34 — a 13× difference. That's not a red flag; it's the formula working as intended (BEAD explicitly weights toward high-cost rural buildout, where running fiber to a handful of remote homes costs far more per location than a dense suburban buildout). The more interesting question this project can eventually answer with more states: does the per-location subsidy actually track real deployment cost, or does it just track politics/formula quirks? Not resolved here — this is a first pass, not a verdict.

Methodology & caveats

  • Ookla regional figures now use a real state-boundary join (fixed from v1.2). Originally filtered by a lon/lat bounding box, which could pick up border slivers from neighboring states. Now does a true point-in-polygon join against US Census cartographic boundary shapefiles (via geopandas). Numbers shifted slightly once border noise was excluded (e.g. NC's fixed-tile count dropped from ~124K to ~73K tiles, median down speed nudged up).
  • Starlink and Licensed Fixed Wireless show zero cross-state variance in this dataset. Both are priced from a single national plan compared against an FCC technology-level median that barely moves by state (280/30 Mbps for LEO Satellite, 25/3 Mbps for Licensed Fixed Wireless, in literally every one of the 10 states). The resulting $0.196 vs. $2.00 numbers are exactly identical everywhere — a real reflection of national pricing and a fairly flat FCC-reported speed tier, not 10 independently-arrived-at confirmations of the same ratio. Fiber, Cable, GSO Satellite, and DSL vary meaningfully by state because their real per-state median speeds do.
  • A small "Other" technology bucket appears in 4 of the 10 states. CA (4 locations), FL (3), NY (1), and TX (867) have a handful of locations under an FCC technology code outside the 8 mapped here. Negligible in every case (never above ~0.01% of a state's locations) — noted rather than silently dropped, but not worth a dedicated investigation at this size.
  • BEAD allocation figures are pre-restructuring and materially wrong for at least 3 verified states (see callout above). The June 2023 initial allocation was superseded by NTIA's 2025 "Benefit of the Bargain" restructuring; confirmed actual awards are dramatically lower for NJ (-76%), TX (~-62%), and CO (~-52%) so far. This isn't a minor staleness caveat — the underlying program changed. NTIA's own allocation-totals page blocks automated fetching, so even the original figures were sourced via secondary aggregators rather than pulled programmatically. A full re-verification against confirmed final/provisional awards for every state is the next planned step for this layer, not optional polish. GSO Satellite locations served is used as an "underserved" proxy, not a precise BEAD-eligibility count (a location can have both satellite and another option available) — grounded in BEAD's own treatment of satellite as non-qualifying/lower-tier service, but still an approximation, and this is a first pass at the policy layer, not a finished analysis.
  • Advertised vs. FCC-median speed gap. FCC's LEO Satellite median (280/30 Mbps) is higher than any individual Starlink residential pricing tier in the dataset (which top out at 400 Mbps for $130; the common $55 tier is only 100 Mbps). The $0.196/Mbps figure is cheapest plan price ÷ FCC's fleet-wide median speed, not an apples-to-apples plan-to-speed match — both columns are kept visible in the table above rather than resolved silently.
  • NJ DSL is degenerate. FCC's reported median download speed for DSL in NJ rounds to 0 Mbps across 65,253 locations — almost certainly legacy copper barely providing real service, not a data error. Cost-per-Mbps is mathematically undefined (division by zero) and shown as such in the table rather than smoothed over.
  • Fixed Wireless is three technologies, not one. FCC splits it into Unlicensed (code 70), Licensed (71), and Licensed-by-Rule/LBR (72). An earlier version of this page collapsed all three into one "Fixed Wireless" row and let 71/72 fall into an unlabeled "Other" bucket — which mattered, since Licensed Fixed Wireless (the actual technology behind Verizon 5G Home / T-Mobile Home Internet) turned out to be the single largest fixed-wireless population by far in every state. Now split and priced correctly; LBR has no matching published pricing in any state, so it's shown in the table but excluded from the range chart and left unpriced rather than researched for a niche product.
  • Pricing is a snapshot, not a full market. One or two representative plans per technology (cheapest published tier), not an exhaustive rate survey; national averages fill gaps where a specific regional provider wasn't researched.

Personal reliability layer — in progress

A lightweight logger (Cloudflare speed-test endpoints + ping, no external dependencies) is running every 30 minutes on two machines — a home broadband connection and a separate cellular-capable connection — logging download/upload throughput, latency, jitter, and packet loss to a local database. Satellite is intentionally out of scope for this layer for now (no satellite hardware access); it stays in the market-layer analysis above. This needs a few weeks of runway before the personal-vs-market overlay is meaningful — check back for an update once there's enough data to say something real.
