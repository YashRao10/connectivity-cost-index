The question

Satellite broadband carries a well-known reputation for being expensive relative to its speed — a "tax" paid for coverage in places wired options can't reach. This project tests that claim with public data instead of assuming it: FCC's National Broadband Map (which technologies actually serve which locations, split by GSO vs. LEO satellite) joined against published ISP/carrier pricing, across a dense metro (NJ), a suburban-mix state (NC), and a rural/satellite-dependent state (MT).

The finding complicates the premise, and gets more interesting the deeper the fixed-wireless category gets split apart. LEO satellite (Starlink) is not the worst value in any of the three states — it beats legacy GSO satellite, DSL, and every flavor of fixed wireless everywhere, and only loses to cable and fiber where those are actually available. The sharpest result: Starlink beats Licensed Fixed Wireless (Verizon 5G Home / T-Mobile Home Internet — the actual 5G-home-internet product, not a niche one) by roughly 10× on cost-per-Mbps, $0.196 vs. $2.00, in all three states. The real story is less "satellite is a tax" and more "satellite beats most of its terrestrial wireless competition outright, and loses only to wired infrastructure where it exists."

These are blended medians across every technology serving a state — not "fiber's real speed" or "satellite's real speed" specifically. Mobile is meaningfully slower than fixed everywhere, and MT (rural) has the highest mobile latency (49ms) and lowest mobile test volume by far — consistent with a thinner cellular buildout.

Methodology & caveats

  • Ookla regional figures use a bounding-box approximation, not true state boundaries. Tiles were filtered by lon/lat centroid box per state, which can pick up border slivers from neighboring states (e.g. NJ's box touches NY/PA/DE edges). Good enough for a directional read; not precise enough to cite as an exact per-state number. A real fix needs a spatial join against Census state shapefiles.
  • Advertised vs. FCC-median speed gap. FCC's LEO Satellite median (280/30 Mbps) is higher than any individual Starlink residential pricing tier in the dataset (which top out at 400 Mbps for $130; the common $55 tier is only 100 Mbps). The $0.196/Mbps figure is cheapest plan price ÷ FCC's fleet-wide median speed, not an apples-to-apples plan-to-speed match — both columns are kept visible in the table above rather than resolved silently.
  • NJ DSL is degenerate. FCC's reported median download speed for DSL in NJ rounds to 0 Mbps across 65,253 locations — almost certainly legacy copper barely providing real service, not a data error. Cost-per-Mbps is mathematically undefined (division by zero) and shown as such in the table rather than smoothed over.
  • Fixed Wireless is three technologies, not one. FCC splits it into Unlicensed (code 70), Licensed (71), and Licensed-by-Rule/LBR (72). An earlier version of this page collapsed all three into one "Fixed Wireless" row and let 71/72 fall into an unlabeled "Other" bucket — which mattered, since Licensed Fixed Wireless (the actual technology behind Verizon 5G Home / T-Mobile Home Internet) turned out to be the single largest fixed-wireless population by far (100–1000× the other two). Now split and priced correctly; LBR is small enough in all 3 states (43–47.6K locations) and has no matching published pricing, so it's shown in the table but excluded from the chart and left unpriced rather than researched for a niche product.
  • Pricing is a snapshot, not a full market. One or two representative plans per technology (cheapest published tier), not an exhaustive rate survey; national averages fill gaps where a specific regional provider wasn't researched.

Personal reliability layer — in progress

A lightweight logger (Cloudflare speed-test endpoints + ping, no external dependencies) is running every 30 minutes on two machines — a home broadband connection and a separate cellular-capable connection — logging download/upload throughput, latency, jitter, and packet loss to a local database. Satellite is intentionally out of scope for this layer for now (no satellite hardware access); it stays in the market-layer analysis above. This needs a few weeks of runway before the personal-vs-market overlay is meaningful — check back for an update once there's enough data to say something real.
