The question

Satellite broadband carries a well-known reputation for being expensive relative to its speed, a "tax" paid for coverage in places wired options can't reach. This project tests that claim with public data instead of assuming it: FCC's National Broadband Map (which technologies actually serve which locations, split by GSO vs. LEO satellite) joined against published ISP/carrier pricing. The market layer now covers all 50 states plus DC, every region type, not a sample.

The finding holds at full scale, though it's more nuanced than the earlier 10-state version suggested. LEO satellite (Starlink) beats legacy GSO satellite everywhere (51 of 51) and beats Licensed Fixed Wireless (Verizon 5G Home / T-Mobile Home Internet, the actual 5G-home-internet product, not a niche one) everywhere too (51 of 51), but not always by the same exact 10× margin the smaller sample showed. Most states land at exactly 10× ($0.196 vs. $2.00), but the gap ranges from a modest 2.1× in Idaho ($0.417) up to 25.5× in Alaska ($5.00). It does not beat every other technology everywhere, though: DSL in Rhode Island, and unlicensed fixed wireless pricing in Hawaii, Nevada, and Rhode Island, both come in cheaper than LEO satellite on this metric. These are real exceptions, not smoothed over. The real story is less "satellite always wins" and more "satellite reliably beats its most direct national competitor (licensed 5G home internet) everywhere, while losing occasionally to cheaper legacy/local options and consistently to wired infrastructure where that exists."

Worth saying up front: Starlink's own cost-per-Mbps is flat at $0.196 in every state (one national price against an FCC technology-level median that barely moves), while Licensed Fixed Wireless is not flat at full scale: it varies from $0.417 to $5.00 depending on that state's FCC-reported median speed tier for the technology. So the "beats it everywhere" finding is 51 genuinely different comparisons, not one national ratio repeated 51 times. That's a meaningfully stronger claim than the original 10-state framing, which happened to sample only states where Licensed Fixed Wireless's speed tier was identical. See methodology for the full caveat.

How this data is actually measured: networking basics

Every number below travels the same basic path, and the protocol carrying it changes what "connection quality" even means. Quick primer before the data.

This project measures from the modem outward: ISP pricing, advertised vs. real speeds, subsidy data. Router/Wi-Fi problems (weak signal, old hardware, too many devices) are real but a different problem, and not what any number on this page is testing.

TCP: reliable delivery
Confirms every packet arrived, resends anything lost, keeps everything in order. Used for: web pages, file downloads, email. Tradeoff: one lost packet stalls everything behind it until it's resent. That's the "spinning wheel" moment on a slow connection.
UDP: speed over guarantees
Sends packets and moves on. No confirmation, no resending. Used for: video calls, online gaming, and live streaming, where a late packet is worthless anyway. Tradeoff: a lost packet is just gone. You get a frozen video frame, not a delay.

Correction: Ookla's Speedtest, which the real-world performance layer below is built on, actually measures throughput over TCP (multiple parallel connections), not UDP. That's a deliberate choice, since TCP's own congestion-control behavior is closer to what a real download or upload actually feels like than a raw UDP probe would be. Latency/jitter/packet-loss are measured as a separate, lighter-weight check. Not every speed-test tool works this way, but Ookla specifically does.

This is why latency (round-trip delay, in ms), jitter (how much that delay swings from packet to packet), and packet loss (% of packets that never arrive), all three shown in the personal reliability layer further down, matter differently depending on the protocol. TCP mostly hides jitter and loss behind retries; you just see a page load slower. UDP doesn't hide it. That's the frozen call or the game lag spike, which is exactly why real-time apps use it instead of TCP.

How each network actually reaches you

Seven technologies show up in the data on this page. They move bits in physically different ways, and that explains most of the price, speed, and latency gaps before any market effect kicks in. Start with the biggest one: how far a satellite signal has to travel.

GSO satellite latency floor
~477 ms
Physics alone. Your request goes up to the satellite and down to a gateway, and the reply makes the same trip back: 4 × 35,786 km at the speed of light. Real connections add processing on top.
LEO satellite latency floor
~7 ms
Same four legs at ~550 km, with the satellite straight overhead. Real-world numbers run higher (satellites are rarely directly above you, and traffic still routes through a ground gateway), but the starting point is about 65× lower.
Fiber latency floor
~1 ms / 100 km
Light in glass moves at roughly two-thirds of its vacuum speed. For wired networks the distance that matters is how far away the server is, not how high up anything is.

Change from the 2023 allocation to the 2025 award, one column per state. Every state was cut. Hover for dollar amounts.

The federal BEAD program allocated $42.45B across states in 2023. In 2025, NTIA restructured the program ("Benefit of the Bargain"); this layer shows both the original 2023 allocation and the 2025 provisional award for all 50 states + DC. The provisional award for 50 of 51 is individually verified against NTIA's own official per-state "BEAD Final Proposal Overview" PDF (broadbandusa.ntia.gov). DC is the one exception, since no such PDF has been published for it, so DC keeps an earlier secondary-source estimate, flagged as unverified (see methodology). As a proxy for "underserved" locations, GSO Satellite locations served (from the FCC layer above) stands in. BEAD's own eligibility rules treat satellite as non-qualifying/lower-tier service in most cases, so places with heavy GSO satellite coverage plausibly correlate with places lacking adequate terrestrial options.

The restructuring cut funding nationally, not just in the handful of states first checked. Median cut across all 50 states + DC: −62.0% vs. the original 2023 allocation. The range is wide: Oregon (−14.7%), Illinois (−16.9%), and California (−25.5%) came through relatively unscathed; DC (−99.0%), North Dakota (−95.3%), and Connecticut (−95.0%) were cut nearly to zero. This is confirmed structural, not a data artifact. See methodology for sourcing.

Scale sanity-check, not a real cost comparison: the last column shows how many months of the cheapest current Starlink residential plan ($55/mo) each state's $-per-location figure would buy. A mixed project counts under every technology it actually funds, which is the point (it's why the split exists). Different states fund very different technology mixes (Alaska is 51.8% fiber, 33.9% LEO satellite; most East Coast states are 90%+ fiber), and fiber costs 5.6x more per location everywhere. Methodology & caveats

19 notes, click any card to read it
Caveats to keep in mind 8
Starlink's cost-per-Mbps is flat nationwide; Licensed Fixed Wireless's is not, once the sample grew past 10 states.

Starlink is priced from a single national plan against an FCC technology-level median that barely moves (280/30 Mbps in all 51 states/DC), so its $0.196/Mbps is genuinely identical everywhere. The original 10-state sample happened to show Licensed Fixed Wireless at a flat $2.00 too, which looked like the same mechanical pattern, but at full 51-state scale it actually varies $0.417 (Idaho) to $5.00 (Alaska), because that technology's FCC-reported speed tier isn't as nationally uniform as Starlink's. The "beats it everywhere" finding is therefore stronger now, not weaker: 51 genuinely different comparisons landing the same direction, not one ratio repeated.

LEO satellite does not beat every technology everywhere.

DSL in Rhode Island ($0.05/Mbps) and unlicensed fixed wireless pricing in Hawaii, Nevada, and Rhode Island (as low as $0.045/Mbps) all come in cheaper than Starlink's $0.196/Mbps on this metric. These are real exceptions surfaced by the full-state expansion, not present in the original 10-state sample, and are not smoothed over here.

A small "Other" technology bucket appears in 15 of the 51 states/DC.

Locations under an FCC technology code outside the 8 mapped here, largest in TX (867 locations). Negligible in every case (never above ~0.001% of a state's locations), so it's noted rather than silently dropped, but not worth a dedicated investigation at this size.

Advertised vs. FCC-median speed gap.

FCC's LEO Satellite median (280/30 Mbps) is higher than any individual Starlink residential pricing tier in the dataset (which top out at 400 Mbps for $130; the common $55 tier is only 100 Mbps). The $0.196/Mbps figure is cheapest plan price ÷ FCC's fleet-wide median speed, not an apples-to-apples plan-to-speed match. Both columns are kept visible in the table above rather than resolved silently.

DSL is degenerate in 2 states.

FCC's reported median download speed for DSL rounds to 0 Mbps in NJ (65,253 locations) and VA (271,861 locations), almost certainly legacy copper barely providing real service, not a data error. Cost-per-Mbps is mathematically undefined (division by zero) in both and shown as such in the table rather than smoothed over.

Fixed Wireless is three technologies, not one.

FCC splits it into Unlicensed (code 70), Licensed (71), and Licensed-by-Rule/LBR (72). An earlier version of this page collapsed all three into one "Fixed Wireless" row and let 71/72 fall into an unlabeled "Other" bucket, which mattered, since Licensed Fixed Wireless (the actual technology behind Verizon 5G Home / T-Mobile Home Internet) turned out to be the single largest fixed-wireless population by far in every state. Now split and priced correctly; LBR has no matching published pricing in any state, so it's shown in the table but excluded from the range chart and left unpriced rather than researched for a niche product.

Pricing is a snapshot, not a full market.

One or two representative plans per technology (cheapest published tier), not an exhaustive rate survey; national averages fill gaps where a specific regional provider wasn't researched.

Provider names in the pricing data are price sources, not local availability.

Each technology is priced from one cheapest published plan and that price is used in every state (Verizon Fios for fiber, Spectrum for cable, and so on). So a row reading "Verizon Fios" in Alaska means the fiber price came from Fios's national plan, not that Fios sells service there. What varies by state is the FCC-reported median speed, which is why each state's cost per Mbps differs. State-specific provider pricing would be a real improvement and hasn't been done yet.

The nationwide Ookla tile files were already local; expanding coverage only required widening COMPARISON_STATES in load_ookla_regional.py, no new downloads. Alaska overtook Montana as the slowest/highest-latency state once added (148.7 Mbps median fixed download, 61ms mobile latency), a genuinely new finding from the expanded sample, not visible at 36 states.

BEAD layer rebuilt with confirmed 2025 provisional awards for all 50 states + DC (v2, resolves the earlier 10-state staleness flag).

Both the original June 2023 allocation and the 2025 "Benefit of the Bargain" provisional award are hand-compiled from a single consistent secondary source (NTIA's own allocation-totals page blocks automated fetching), cross-checked against directly-sourced NTIA/state-broadband-office figures for NJ, TX, and CO during the earlier caveat fix (all matched within ~1-2%). "Provisional" means administrative review is still ongoing per state as of compilation, so these are not guaranteed to be each state's final locked-in number, and should be re-verified before citing elsewhere. GSO Satellite locations served remains an "underserved" proxy, not a precise BEAD-eligibility count. See docs/BEAD_LAYER_SCOPE.md for the full reasoning.

Added a Starlink-cost cross-reference to the BEAD table (2026-09-22).

Each state's $-per-location figure is now also shown as months of the cheapest current Starlink residential plan ($55/mo, sourced the same way build_cost_comparison.py picks a representative price). This is explicitly not a claim that the subsidy could substitute for a Starlink plan. BEAD funds one-time infrastructure, not a subscription. But it's a useful scale check: even Alaska's outlier (originally $784.17/location on the pre-verification figures, now $1,021.06/location once NTIA-verified; see below) is under 2 years of consumer satellite service, which is a strong signal that the per-location dollar figure is driven mostly by the proxy's oversized denominator (nearly every location "has satellite coverage") rather than by real last-mile deployment cost. See scripts/build_bead_comparison.py.

Added a granular BEAD layer with real per-project technology splits (2026-09-22), replacing the GSO-satellite-coverage proxy with actual funded-location data.

NTIA's own site has no bulk Final Proposal download, so this uses a third-party compilation (broadbandexpanded.com) of 5,806 real subgrantee projects, each with an actual federal subsidy amount and a location-level technology list. ~5.7% of projects fund more than one technology; the dollar figure is split across technologies proportional to each one's share of that project's locations, not assigned whole to one (checked directly: 290 of 5,093 sampled projects had >1 technology). Headline: satellite (GSO+LEO) is 22.0% of all funded locations at $701–$1,138/location vs. fiber's $6,420.70/location (63.3% of locations), a real 5.6× cost gap, not a proxy estimate. Reconciled against this site's existing state-total figures: 40 of 50 states agree within 15%, 10 don't (worst: DC −83% on a tiny base, HI −68%, AK +30%): no single cause confirmed, flagged rather than resolved. See scripts/build_bead_final_proposal.py and the new section above for the full caveats.

Choropleth map now plots all 50 states + DC (v3.4), not just the contiguous 48 + DC.

Alaska and Hawaii were previously excluded from the map's rendering (though not from any table or the underlying data) over antimeridian-wrap and disconnected-islands concerns. The map already used d3's AlbersUSA projection, which solves exactly that problem by repositioning AK/HI as bottom-left insets, so the exclusion was unnecessary. Alaska turns out to be the map's actual most subsidy-intense state ($784/location), which the map's legend previously couldn't show since AK wasn't plotted.

Personal reliability layer (in progress)

A lightweight logger (Cloudflare speed-test endpoints + ping, no external dependencies) is running every 30 minutes on two machines, each on its own home broadband connection, logging download/upload throughput, latency, jitter, and packet loss to a local database. The logger also supports labeling a session as a tethered/cellular connection (--label cellular-tether), but no cellular data has actually been collected yet. Both machines are on home Wi-Fi so far. Satellite is intentionally out of scope for this layer for now (no satellite hardware access); it stays in the market-layer analysis above. This needs a few weeks of runway before the personal-vs-market overlay is meaningful. Check back for an update once there's enough data to say something real.
