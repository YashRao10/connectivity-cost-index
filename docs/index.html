    Satellite broadband carries a well-known reputation for being expensive relative to its speed, a "tax" paid for coverage in places wired options can't reach. This project tests that claim with public data instead of assuming it: FCC's National Broadband Map (which technologies actually serve which locations, split by GSO vs. LEO satellite) joined against published ISP/carrier pricing. The market layer covers all 50 states plus DC, every region type, not a sample.

    The finding holds across every state, with some nuance. LEO satellite (Starlink) beats legacy GSO satellite everywhere (51 of 51) and beats Licensed Fixed Wireless (Verizon 5G Home / T-Mobile Home Internet, the actual 5G-home-internet product, not a niche one) everywhere too (51 of 51), though not by the same margin everywhere. Most states (47 of 51) land at about 10× ($0.196 vs. $2.00), but the gap ranges from a modest 2.1× in Idaho ($0.417) up to 25.5× in Alaska ($5.00). It does not beat every other technology everywhere, though: unlicensed fixed wireless in Hawaii, Nevada, and Rhode Island, and DSL in Delaware and Rhode Island, come in cheaper than LEO satellite on this metric (the two DSL cases cover only 4 and 14 FCC-reported locations). These are real exceptions, not smoothed over. The real story is less "satellite always wins" and more "satellite reliably beats its most direct national competitor (licensed 5G home internet) everywhere, while losing occasionally to cheaper legacy/local options and consistently to wired infrastructure where that exists."

    Worth saying up front: Starlink's own cost-per-Mbps is flat at $0.196 in every state (one national price against an FCC technology-level median that barely moves), while Licensed Fixed Wireless is not flat: it varies from $0.417 to $5.00 depending on that state's FCC-reported median speed tier for the technology. So the "beats it everywhere" finding is 51 genuinely different comparisons, not one national ratio repeated 51 times. See methodology for the full caveat.

    Part 2How the networks work

    A short primer: how each technology physically reaches a home, and which part of the connection this page actually measures.

    How each network actually reaches you

    Seven technologies show up in the data on this page. They move bits in physically different ways, and that explains most of the price, speed, and latency gaps before any market effect kicks in. Start with the biggest one: how far a satellite signal has to travel.

    GSO satellite latency floor
    ~477 ms
    Physics alone. Your request goes up to the satellite and down to a gateway, and the reply makes the same trip back: 4 × 35,786 km at the speed of light. Real connections add processing on top.
    LEO satellite latency floor
    ~7 ms
    Same four legs at ~550 km, with the satellite straight overhead. Real-world numbers run higher (satellites are rarely directly above you, and traffic still routes through a ground gateway), but the starting point is about 65× lower.
    Fiber latency floor
    ~1 ms / 100 km
    Light in glass moves at roughly two-thirds of its vacuum speed. For wired networks the distance that matters is how far away the server is, not how high up anything is.

    The seven technologies, side by side. Numbers come from the same national data used everywhere else on this page: the FCC-reported median download speed and cheapest-plan cost per Mbps (each the median across all 50 states + DC), plus the real BEAD subsidy committed per funded location.

    How this data is actually measured: networking basics

    Every number below travels the same basic path, and the protocol carrying it changes what "connection quality" even means. Quick primer before the data.

    This project measures from the modem outward: ISP pricing, advertised vs. real speeds, subsidy data. Router/Wi-Fi problems (weak signal, old hardware, too many devices) are real but a different problem, and not what any number on this page is testing.

    TCP: reliable delivery
    Confirms every packet arrived, resends anything lost, keeps everything in order. Used for: web pages, file downloads, email. Tradeoff: one lost packet stalls everything behind it until it's resent. That's the "spinning wheel" moment on a slow connection.
    UDP: speed over guarantees
    Sends packets and moves on. No confirmation, no resending. Used for: video calls, online gaming, and live streaming, where a late packet is worthless anyway. Tradeoff: a lost packet is just gone. You get a frozen video frame, not a delay.

    Worth knowing: Ookla's Speedtest, which the real-world performance layer below is built on, actually measures throughput over TCP (multiple parallel connections), not UDP. That's a deliberate choice, since TCP's own congestion-control behavior is closer to what a real download or upload actually feels like than a raw UDP probe would be. Latency/jitter/packet-loss are measured as a separate, lighter-weight check. Not every speed-test tool works this way, but Ookla specifically does.

    This is why latency (round-trip delay, in ms), jitter (how much that delay swings from packet to packet), and packet loss (% of packets that never arrive), all three shown in the personal reliability layer further down, matter differently depending on the protocol. TCP mostly hides jitter and loss behind retries; you just see a page load slower. UDP doesn't hide it. That's the frozen call or the game lag spike, which is exactly why real-time apps use it instead of TCP.

    Part 7What's next, and the fine print

    A first-hand layer still collecting data, and every caveat and correction.

    Personal reliability layer (in progress)

    The one layer on this page that's measured first-hand instead of pulled from a public dataset. Here's how a sample gets made.

    Download / upload
    Mbps
    Time to pull 10 MB from and push 5 MB to Cloudflare's speed-test servers.
    Latency
    ms
    Average round trip across 10 pings to Cloudflare's 1.1.1.1.
    Jitter
    ms
    How much those 10 round trips swing: the standard deviation of the ping times.
    Packet loss
    %
    Share of the 10 pings that never came back.
    A lightweight logger (Cloudflare speed-test endpoints + ping, no separate speed-test app needed) is running every 30 minutes on two machines, each on its own home broadband connection, logging download/upload throughput, latency, jitter, and packet loss to a local database. The logger also supports labeling a session as a tethered/cellular connection (--label cellular-tether), but no cellular data has actually been collected yet. Both machines are on home Wi-Fi so far. Satellite is intentionally out of scope for this layer for now (no satellite hardware access); it stays in the market-layer analysis above. This needs a few weeks of runway before the personal-vs-market overlay is meaningful. Check back for an update once there's enough data to say something real.

    Methodology & caveats

    19 notes, click any card to read it
    Caveats to keep in mind 8
    Starlink's cost-per-Mbps is flat nationwide; Licensed Fixed Wireless's is not.

    Starlink is priced from a single national plan against an FCC technology-level median that barely moves (280/30 Mbps in all 51 states/DC), so its $0.196/Mbps is genuinely identical everywhere. The original 10-state sample happened to show Licensed Fixed Wireless at a flat $2.00 too, which looked like the same mechanical pattern, but at full 51-state scale it actually varies $0.417 (Idaho) to $5.00 (Alaska), because that technology's FCC-reported speed tier isn't as nationally uniform as Starlink's. The "beats it everywhere" finding is therefore stronger now, not weaker: 51 genuinely different comparisons landing the same direction, not one ratio repeated.

    LEO satellite does not beat every technology everywhere.

    Unlicensed fixed wireless in Hawaii, Nevada, and Rhode Island (as low as $0.045/Mbps) and DSL in Rhode Island ($0.04/Mbps) and Delaware ($0.178/Mbps) all come in cheaper than Starlink's $0.196/Mbps on this metric. The two DSL cases are real rows in the FCC data but cover only 14 (RI) and 4 (DE) locations, with a reported median speed far above normal DSL, so they say more about FCC reporting than about the DSL market. Shown rather than smoothed over.

    A small "Other" technology bucket appears in 15 of the 51 states/DC.

    Locations under an FCC technology code outside the 8 mapped here, largest in TX (867 locations). Negligible in every case (never above ~0.001% of a state's locations), so it's noted rather than silently dropped, but not worth a dedicated investigation at this size.

    Advertised vs. FCC-median speed gap.

    FCC's LEO Satellite median (280/30 Mbps) is higher than any individual Starlink residential pricing tier in the dataset (which top out at 400 Mbps for $130; the common $55 tier is only 100 Mbps). The $0.196/Mbps figure is cheapest plan price ÷ FCC's fleet-wide median speed, not an apples-to-apples plan-to-speed match. Both columns are kept visible in the table above rather than resolved silently.

    DSL is degenerate in 2 states.

    FCC's reported median download speed for DSL rounds to 0 Mbps in NJ (65,253 locations) and VA (271,861 locations), almost certainly legacy copper barely providing real service, not a data error. Cost-per-Mbps is mathematically undefined (division by zero) in both and shown as such in the table rather than smoothed over.

    Fixed Wireless is three technologies, not one.

    FCC splits it into Unlicensed (code 70), Licensed (71), and Licensed-by-Rule/LBR (72). An earlier version of this page collapsed all three into one "Fixed Wireless" row and let 71/72 fall into an unlabeled "Other" bucket, which mattered, since Licensed Fixed Wireless (the actual technology behind Verizon 5G Home / T-Mobile Home Internet) turned out to be the single largest fixed-wireless population by far in every state. Now split and priced correctly; LBR has no matching published pricing in any state, so it's shown in the table but excluded from the range chart and left unpriced rather than researched for a niche product.

    Pricing is a snapshot, not a full market.

    One cheapest published plan per provider (as of September 2026), not an exhaustive rate survey. Some of those prices are introductory rates that rise after 12 to 24 months; data/provider_pricing.csv notes which. Taxes, equipment, and modem fees are left out, which matches how providers advertise but understates the real monthly bill.

    Fiber, cable, and DSL use each state's largest provider; the rest use a national plan.

    For fiber, cable, and DSL, the price comes from the provider with the largest coverage for that technology in each state (per BroadbandNow's state pages), so Texas fiber is priced from AT&T Fiber and Oregon's from Ziply. Coverage figures there are a provider's total footprint, so a provider is counted under the technology it lists first; AT&T's combined listing is counted as fiber, since AT&T no longer sells DSL to new customers, and resellers like EarthLink are excluded. AT&T stopped selling DSL to new customers in 2023, so where AT&T is the largest DSL provider, DSL is priced from the next-largest provider still selling it (Brightspeed, CenturyLink, or Frontier), as long as that provider covers at least 2% of the state. That leaves 6 states (CA, GA, HI, KY, OK, TN) on the national DSL average. Starlink, Viasat, Verizon 5G Home, and T-Mobile Home Internet are sold at one national price, so those always use it. Provider picks and prices are in data/state_top_providers.csv and data/provider_pricing.csv.

    Corrections found by checking the data 6
    Ookla regional figures now use a real state-boundary join.

    Originally filtered by a lon/lat bounding box, which could pick up border slivers from neighboring states. Now does a true point-in-polygon join against US Census cartographic boundary shapefiles (via geopandas). Numbers shifted slightly once border noise was excluded (e.g. NC's fixed-tile count dropped from ~124K to ~73K tiles, median down speed nudged up).

    Q1→Q2 trend expanded to all 50 states + DC, correcting an overstated finding.

    The 10-state sample's "near-universal decline, Florida the one exception" framing didn't hold at full scale: 82 of 102 state/network combinations declined (80.4%, not effectively 90%+), and 19 combinations actually increased, including Arizona, DC mobile, and Wyoming mobile, not just Florida. Required downloading Q1 2026's raw tile files (they weren't kept locally after the original 10-state run) since build_ookla_timeseries.py needs the actual prior-quarter tiles, not just the current summary CSV.

    Replaced BEAD provisional-award figures with NTIA-verified numbers, all 50 states + DC.

    The original figures (v2, telecompetitor.com-sourced) checked out unreliable when investigated further: spot-checking 27 states directly against NTIA's own official per-state "BEAD Final Proposal Overview" PDFs (broadbandusa.ntia.gov, a primary source with no bulk download, so each state's current PDF path had to be looked up individually via its NTIA "awardee" page, since the URL's date-folder drifts as proposals get amended) found only 12 of 27 matched within 2%; Hawaii was 3× overstated ($94.87M shown vs. the real $30.67M). Re-verified all 50 states + DC this way: 50 states now carry a real NTIA figure; DC is the one exception (no overview PDF exists for it) and keeps the old unverified estimate, flagged as such.     Fixed a data-completeness gap in the granular BEAD layer: 56 projects ($88.0M) with no location data were being silently dropped.

    build_bead_final_proposal.py only counted a project if it had matching rows in LOCATION.csv: 56 projects nationally don't, most consequentially South Dakota's single largest project ($72.8M), which previously zeroed out SD's entire presence in this layer (it simply didn't appear in the table), and one DC project ($827K) that was most of why DC's granular total looked implausibly tiny. Found while root-causing the RI/NC/IL reconciliation gaps (which turned out to have a different, unrelated cause; see below). This didn't change the headline satellite-vs-fiber finding (still 22.0% / $6,420.70 per location / 5.6×) since $88M is under 0.5% of the $18.47B total, but it did fix two states that were previously misleadingly absent or incomplete.

    Root-caused the RI/NC BEAD reconciliation gaps: both match an old, superseded secondary-source figure exactly, not a live data error.

    Rhode Island's and North Carolina's granular project-level totals ($16,137,983 and $408,511,175.10) match the original, since-replaced telecompetitor.com figures for those states, to the dollar: strong evidence that broadbandexpanded.com's underlying project data for RI and NC reflects an earlier BEAD submission than the one NTIA's current overview PDF shows, rather than a bug in this layer's aggregation.     Illinois's reconciliation gap ruled out as a duplicate-counting or stale-data bug; real cause is a technology-mix/location-count mismatch, not fully explained.

    Ookla layer expanded to all 50 states + DC, matching the market layer.

    The nationwide Ookla tile files were already local; expanding coverage only required widening COMPARISON_STATES in load_ookla_regional.py, no new downloads. Alaska overtook Montana as the slowest/highest-latency state once added (148.7 Mbps median fixed download, 61ms mobile latency), a genuinely new finding from the expanded sample, not visible at 36 states.

    BEAD layer rebuilt with confirmed 2025 provisional awards for all 50 states + DC.

    (Since superseded: the provisional-award figures were later re-verified against NTIA's own PDFs; see Corrections.) Both the original June 2023 allocation and the 2025 "Benefit of the Bargain" provisional award are hand-compiled from a single consistent secondary source (NTIA's own allocation-totals page blocks automated fetching), cross-checked against directly-sourced NTIA/state-broadband-office figures for NJ, TX, and CO during the earlier caveat fix (all matched within ~1-2%). "Provisional" means administrative review is still ongoing per state as of compilation, so these are not guaranteed to be each state's final locked-in number, and should be re-verified before citing elsewhere. GSO Satellite locations served remains an "underserved" proxy, not a precise BEAD-eligibility count. See docs/BEAD_LAYER_SCOPE.md for the full reasoning.

    Added a Starlink-cost cross-reference to the BEAD table.

    Each state's $-per-location figure is now also shown as months of the cheapest current Starlink residential plan ($55/mo, sourced the same way build_cost_comparison.py picks a representative price). This is explicitly not a claim that the subsidy could substitute for a Starlink plan. BEAD funds one-time infrastructure, not a subscription. But it's a useful scale check: even Alaska's outlier (originally $784.17/location on the pre-verification figures, now $1,021.06/location once NTIA-verified; see below) is under 2 years of consumer satellite service, which is a strong signal that the per-location dollar figure is driven mostly by the proxy's oversized denominator (nearly every location "has satellite coverage") rather than by real last-mile deployment cost. See scripts/build_bead_comparison.py.

    Added a granular BEAD layer with real per-project technology splits, replacing the GSO-satellite-coverage proxy with actual funded-location data.

    NTIA's own site has no bulk Final Proposal download, so this uses a third-party compilation (broadbandexpanded.com) of 5,806 real subgrantee projects, each with an actual federal subsidy amount and a location-level technology list. ~5.7% of projects fund more than one technology; the dollar figure is split across technologies proportional to each one's share of that project's locations, not assigned whole to one (checked directly: 290 of 5,093 sampled projects had >1 technology). Headline: satellite (GSO+LEO) is 22.0% of all funded locations at $701–$1,138/location vs. fiber's $6,420.70/location (63.3% of locations), a real 5.6× cost gap, not a proxy estimate. Reconciled against the NTIA-verified state totals: 49 of 51 states + DC agree within 15%; the two exceptions (RI, NC) are explained in the corrections above. See scripts/build_bead_final_proposal.py and the section above for the full caveats.

    Choropleth map now plots all 50 states + DC, not just the contiguous 48 + DC.

    Alaska and Hawaii were previously excluded from the map's rendering (though not from any table or the underlying data) over antimeridian-wrap and disconnected-islands concerns. The map already used d3's AlbersUSA projection, which solves exactly that problem by repositioning AK/HI as bottom-left insets, so the exclusion was unnecessary. Alaska turns out to be the map's most subsidy-intense state ($1,021/location on NTIA-verified figures), which the map's legend previously couldn't show since AK wasn't plotted.
