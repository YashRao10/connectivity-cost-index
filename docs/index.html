The question

Satellite broadband carries a well-known reputation for being expensive relative to its speed — a "tax" paid for coverage in places wired options can't reach. This project tests that claim with public data instead of assuming it: FCC's National Broadband Map (which technologies actually serve which locations, split by GSO vs. LEO satellite) joined against published ISP/carrier pricing. The market layer now covers all 50 states plus DC — every region type, not a sample.

The finding holds at full scale, though it's more nuanced than the earlier 10-state version suggested. LEO satellite (Starlink) beats legacy GSO satellite everywhere (51 of 51) and beats Licensed Fixed Wireless (Verizon 5G Home / T-Mobile Home Internet — the actual 5G-home-internet product, not a niche one) everywhere too (51 of 51) — but not always by the same exact 10× margin the smaller sample showed. Most states land at exactly 10× ($0.196 vs. $2.00), but the gap ranges from a modest 2.1× in Idaho ($0.417) up to 25.5× in Alaska ($5.00). It does not beat every other technology everywhere, though: DSL in Rhode Island, and unlicensed fixed wireless pricing in Hawaii, Nevada, and Rhode Island, both come in cheaper than LEO satellite on this metric — real exceptions, not smoothed over. The real story is less "satellite always wins" and more "satellite reliably beats its most direct national competitor (licensed 5G home internet) everywhere, while losing occasionally to cheaper legacy/local options and consistently to wired infrastructure where that exists."

Worth flagging up front, not buried in methodology: Starlink's own cost-per-Mbps is flat at $0.196 in every state (one national price against an FCC technology-level median that barely moves), while Licensed Fixed Wireless is not flat at full scale — it varies from $0.417 to $5.00 depending on that state's FCC-reported median speed tier for the technology. So the "beats it everywhere" finding is 51 genuinely different comparisons, not one national ratio repeated 51 times — a meaningfully stronger claim than the original 10-state framing, which happened to sample only states where Licensed Fixed Wireless's speed tier was identical. See methodology for the full caveat.

Where the subsidy money goes: BEAD allocation vs. satellite dependence

The federal BEAD program allocated $42.45B across states in 2023. In 2025, NTIA restructured the program ("Benefit of the Bargain"); this layer shows both the original 2023 allocation and the 2025 provisional award for all 50 states + DC. The provisional award for 50 of 51 is individually verified against NTIA's own official per-state "BEAD Final Proposal Overview" PDF (broadbandusa.ntia.gov) — DC is the one exception, no such PDF has been published for it, so DC keeps an earlier secondary-source estimate, flagged as unverified (see methodology). As a proxy for "underserved" locations, GSO Satellite locations served (from the FCC layer above) stands in — BEAD's own eligibility rules treat satellite as non-qualifying/lower-tier service in most cases, so places with heavy GSO satellite coverage plausibly correlate with places lacking adequate terrestrial options.

The restructuring cut funding nationally, not just in the handful of states first checked. Median cut across all 50 states + DC: −62.0% vs. the original 2023 allocation. The range is wide: Oregon (−14.7%), Illinois (−16.9%), and California (−25.5%) came through relatively unscathed; DC (−99.0%), North Dakota (−95.3%), and Connecticut (−95.0%) were cut nearly to zero. This is confirmed structural, not a data artifact — see methodology for sourcing.

The per-location spread is even more dramatic once NTIA-verified: Alaska's provisional award works out to ~$1,021 per GSO-satellite-served location vs. Connecticut's ~$2.21 — a 462× difference (up from the 355× spread the earlier, less-accurate secondary-source figures showed, and the 13× spread the original 10-state sample showed before that). Some of this is the formula working as intended (BEAD weights toward high-cost rural buildout), but a 462× spread is large enough that "does the per-location subsidy track real deployment cost, or mostly track how much each state's award was cut" is still a real, open question — the granular layer below answers a related question (satellite vs. fiber cost per location, nationally) but not this exact one (why does the same technology's implied subsidy vary 462x by state).

Scale sanity-check, not a real cost comparison: the last column shows how many months of the cheapest current Starlink residential plan ($55/mo) each state's $-per-location figure would buy. Even Alaska's outlier $1,021.06/location — the highest in the country — is worth about 18.6 months of a consumer satellite subscription, and most states are under 2 months. This isn't a claim that BEAD money could or should just buy Starlink plans instead — BEAD funds permanent infrastructure, not a recurring service, and the location-count denominator above is already a loose "underserved" proxy, not a precise buildout-cost basis (see Methodology). What it does show: the per-location dollar figure, on its own, is nowhere near what it would cost to actually connect a household to anything, wired or wireless — reinforcing that this number tracks the proxy's denominator (how many locations "have satellite coverage," which is nearly all of them) far more than it tracks real deployment economics.

What BEAD money actually builds: real per-project technology mix

Everything above uses GSO Satellite coverage as a proxy for "underserved locations" because state-total allocation figures don't say what technology the money actually funds. NTIA's BEAD Final Proposal data does: 5,806 real subgrantee projects, each with an actual federal subsidy amount and a list of specific locations with the technology planned for each one. This section replaces the proxy with those real numbers — project-level dollars, split across technologies by each project's actual location mix (~5.7% of projects fund more than one technology, e.g. mostly fiber with a handful of satellite locations for the hardest-to-reach spots — the $ figure is allocated proportionally to each technology's share of that project's locations, not assigned whole to one). Source: a third-party compilation of NTIA's own Final Proposal submissions (broadbandexpanded.com, since NTIA has no single bulk download) — the compiler itself flags this as "not intended to be authoritative." National totals here ($18.47B) now run within 1.8% of the sum of this site's own state-total figures ($18.15B) once those were individually verified against NTIA's official per-state overview PDFs (see the BEAD table above and methodology) — the two independent sources agree closely at the national level, which is reassuring, though individual states can still diverge (Illinois: $831.2M verified vs. $935.8M in this granular layer, a gap not yet explained).

This answers the question the original proxy couldn't: satellite (GSO + LEO combined) accounts for 22.0% of all BEAD-funded locations nationally — 833,094 of 3,780,117 — at a real committed cost of $701–$1,138 per location, vs. $6,420.70 per location for fiber (63.3% of funded locations). That's a genuine 5.6× gap in actual dollars, not a proxy estimate. BEAD is leaning on satellite at real scale, not just where fiber is geographically impossible — and largely via Starlink specifically (Space Exploration Technologies Corp/SpaceX subgrantee entries outnumber Amazon Kuiper's roughly 7:1 in LEO-funded project locations in this dataset; Kuiper is present too, not hypothetical).

"Projects" here counts distinct projects with at least one location of that technology, not a mutually-exclusive split — a mixed project counts under every technology it actually funds, which is the point (it's why the split exists). Fiber's real per-location cost ($6,420.70) sitting an order of magnitude above every wireless/satellite option is the number this whole layer was built to find — it's the actual answer to whether the state-total $/location spread (CT $2.21 to AK $1,021.06, above) tracks real buildout cost: partially. The 462x state-level spread is real and NTIA-verified, not a data artifact, but this national technology-level breakdown shows it isn't simply "some states pay more for the same thing" — different states fund very different technology mixes (Alaska is 51.8% fiber, 33.9% LEO satellite; most East Coast states are 90%+ fiber), and fiber costs 5.6x more per location everywhere. Both effects are real and stack.

Data-completeness fix (2026-09-23): 56 BEAD projects nationally ($88.0M combined) have zero matching rows in the location-level data — a scope gap in the source compilation, not something attributable by technology. Dropping them would have silently understated a few states' totals, most severely South Dakota (one $72.8M project with no location rows, which previously zeroed out SD's entire presence in this layer) and DC (one $827K project, previously most of why DC looked incomplete here). Both are now correctly represented, shown in the "Unknown (no location data)" row above rather than vanishing.

Reconciliation update (2026-09-23): the state-total BEAD figures above were individually re-verified against NTIA's own official per-state overview PDFs (50 of 51 states directly confirmed; DC has no such PDF published, flagged unverified) — this superseded the original secondary-source figures, which checked out unreliable (only 12 of 27 spot-checked states matched NTIA's real numbers within 2%; Hawaii was 3x overstated). Against those now-verified figures, this granular layer's own state totals agree within 15% for 49 of all 51 states + DC. Root cause found for the two exceptions: Rhode Island's and North Carolina's project-level totals here match the old, superseded telecompetitor.com figures to the dollar ($16,137,983 and $408,511,175.10 respectively) — broadbandexpanded.com's underlying project data for those two states appears to reflect an earlier submission than the one NTIA's current overview PDF shows, not a live-vs-verified mismatch in this layer's own math. Illinois sits just under the flag threshold at +12.6% ($935.8M here vs. $831.2M verified) and remains genuinely unexplained — checked and ruled out the same stale-snapshot pattern that resolved RI/NC. The national technology-level pattern above (fiber vs. satellite cost gap) is far more robust than any single state's number, since it's averaged across thousands of projects rather than one state's total.

Methodology & caveats

  • Ookla regional figures now use a real state-boundary join (fixed from v1.2). Originally filtered by a lon/lat bounding box, which could pick up border slivers from neighboring states. Now does a true point-in-polygon join against US Census cartographic boundary shapefiles (via geopandas). Numbers shifted slightly once border noise was excluded (e.g. NC's fixed-tile count dropped from ~124K to ~73K tiles, median down speed nudged up).
  • Ookla layer expanded to all 50 states + DC (v3.1), matching the market layer. The nationwide Ookla tile files were already local; expanding coverage only required widening COMPARISON_STATES in load_ookla_regional.py, no new downloads. Alaska overtook Montana as the slowest/highest-latency state once added (148.7 Mbps median fixed download, 61ms mobile latency) — a genuinely new finding from the expanded sample, not visible at 36 states.
  • Q1→Q2 trend expanded to all 50 states + DC (v3.3), correcting an overstated finding. The 10-state sample's "near-universal decline, Florida the one exception" framing didn't hold at full scale: 82 of 102 state/network combinations declined (80.4%, not effectively 90%+), and 19 combinations actually increased, including Arizona, DC mobile, and Wyoming mobile — not just Florida. Required downloading Q1 2026's raw tile files (they weren't kept locally after the original 10-state run) since build_ookla_timeseries.py needs the actual prior-quarter tiles, not just the current summary CSV.
  • Starlink's cost-per-Mbps is flat nationwide; Licensed Fixed Wireless's is not, once the sample grew past 10 states. Starlink is priced from a single national plan against an FCC technology-level median that barely moves (280/30 Mbps in all 51 states/DC), so its $0.196/Mbps is genuinely identical everywhere. The original 10-state sample happened to show Licensed Fixed Wireless at a flat $2.00 too, which looked like the same mechanical pattern — but at full 51-state scale it actually varies $0.417 (Idaho) to $5.00 (Alaska), because that technology's FCC-reported speed tier isn't as nationally uniform as Starlink's. The "beats it everywhere" finding is therefore stronger now, not weaker: 51 genuinely different comparisons landing the same direction, not one ratio repeated.
  • LEO satellite does not beat every technology everywhere. DSL in Rhode Island ($0.05/Mbps) and unlicensed fixed wireless pricing in Hawaii, Nevada, and Rhode Island (as low as $0.045/Mbps) all come in cheaper than Starlink's $0.196/Mbps on this metric. These are real exceptions surfaced by the full-state expansion, not present in the original 10-state sample, and are not smoothed over here.
  • A small "Other" technology bucket appears in 15 of the 51 states/DC. Locations under an FCC technology code outside the 8 mapped here, largest in TX (867 locations). Negligible in every case (never above ~0.001% of a state's locations) — noted rather than silently dropped, but not worth a dedicated investigation at this size.
  • BEAD layer rebuilt with confirmed 2025 provisional awards for all 50 states + DC (v2, resolves the earlier 10-state staleness flag). Both the original June 2023 allocation and the 2025 "Benefit of the Bargain" provisional award are hand-compiled from a single consistent secondary source (NTIA's own allocation-totals page blocks automated fetching), cross-checked against directly-sourced NTIA/state-broadband-office figures for NJ, TX, and CO during the earlier caveat fix (all matched within ~1-2%). "Provisional" means administrative review is still ongoing per state as of compilation — these are not guaranteed to be each state's final locked-in number, and should be re-verified before citing elsewhere. GSO Satellite locations served remains an "underserved" proxy, not a precise BEAD-eligibility count — see docs/BEAD_LAYER_SCOPE.md for the full reasoning.
  • Added a Starlink-cost cross-reference to the BEAD table (2026-09-22). Each state's $-per-location figure is now also shown as months of the cheapest current Starlink residential plan ($55/mo, sourced the same way build_cost_comparison.py picks a representative price). This is explicitly not a claim that the subsidy could substitute for a Starlink plan — BEAD funds one-time infrastructure, not a subscription — but it's a useful scale check: even Alaska's outlier (originally $784.17/location on the pre-verification figures, now $1,021.06/location once NTIA-verified — see below) is under 2 years of consumer satellite service, which is a strong signal that the per-location dollar figure is driven mostly by the proxy's oversized denominator (nearly every location "has satellite coverage") rather than by real last-mile deployment cost. See scripts/build_bead_comparison.py.
  • Replaced BEAD provisional-award figures with NTIA-verified numbers, all 50 states + DC (2026-09-23). The original figures (v2, telecompetitor.com-sourced) checked out unreliable when investigated further: spot-checking 27 states directly against NTIA's own official per-state "BEAD Final Proposal Overview" PDFs (broadbandusa.ntia.gov — a primary source with no bulk download, so each state's current PDF path had to be looked up individually via its NTIA "awardee" page, since the URL's date-folder drifts as proposals get amended) found only 12 of 27 matched within 2%; Hawaii was 3x overstated ($94.87M shown vs. the real $30.67M). Re-verified all 50 states + DC this way (a two-machine effort, split 27/23) — 50 states now carry a real NTIA figure, DC is the one exception (no overview PDF exists for it) and keeps the old unverified estimate, flagged as such. This changed the headline spread from 355x (CT $2.21 to AK $784.17) to 462x (CT $2.21 to AK $1,021.06) — Alaska's real number was higher than the old estimate, not lower. See scripts/build_bead_provisional_awards.py for the full sourcing methodology and priority order, and docs/BEAD_LAYER_SCOPE.md for how this was discovered.
  • Fixed a data-completeness gap in the granular BEAD layer: 56 projects ($88.0M) with no location data were being silently dropped (2026-09-23). build_bead_final_proposal.py only counted a project if it had matching rows in LOCATION.csv -- 56 projects nationally don't, most consequentially South Dakota's single largest project ($72.8M), which previously zeroed out SD's entire presence in this layer (it simply didn't appear in the table), and one DC project ($827K) that was most of why DC's granular total looked implausibly tiny. Both now show correctly via a new "Unknown (no location data)" technology row rather than vanishing. Found while root-causing the RI/NC/IL reconciliation gaps (which turned out to have a different, unrelated cause -- see below). This didn't change the headline satellite-vs-fiber finding (still 22.0% / $6,420.70 per location / 5.6x) since $88M is under 0.5% of the $18.47B total, but it did fix two states that were previously misleadingly absent or incomplete. 2 new tests covering the fix.
  • Root-caused the RI/NC BEAD reconciliation gaps: both match an old, superseded secondary-source figure exactly, not a live data error (2026-09-23). Rhode Island's and North Carolina's granular project-level totals ($16,137,983 and $408,511,175.10) match the original, since-replaced telecompetitor.com figures for those states to the dollar -- strong evidence that broadbandexpanded.com's underlying project data for RI and NC reflects an earlier BEAD submission than the one NTIA's current overview PDF shows, rather than a bug in this layer's aggregation. Checked directly against data/raw/bead/PROJECT_20260827.csv, no location-join needed to see it. Illinois does not show this pattern (its raw total doesn't match its old telecompetitor figure either) and remains the one genuinely unexplained exception.
  • Added a granular BEAD layer with real per-project technology splits (2026-09-22), replacing the GSO-satellite-coverage proxy with actual funded-location data. NTIA's own site has no bulk Final Proposal download, so this uses a third-party compilation (broadbandexpanded.com) of 5,806 real subgrantee projects — each with an actual federal subsidy amount and a location-level technology list. ~5.7% of projects fund more than one technology; the dollar figure is split across technologies proportional to each one's share of that project's locations, not assigned whole to one (checked directly: 290 of 5,093 sampled projects had >1 technology). Headline: satellite (GSO+LEO) is 22.0% of all funded locations at $701–$1,138/location vs. fiber's $6,420.70/location (63.3% of locations) — a real 5.6× cost gap, not a proxy estimate. Reconciled against this site's existing state-total figures: 40 of 50 states agree within 15%, 10 don't (worst: DC −83% on a tiny base, HI −68%, AK +30%) — no single cause confirmed, flagged rather than resolved. See scripts/build_bead_final_proposal.py and the new section above for the full caveats.
  • Advertised vs. FCC-median speed gap. FCC's LEO Satellite median (280/30 Mbps) is higher than any individual Starlink residential pricing tier in the dataset (which top out at 400 Mbps for $130; the common $55 tier is only 100 Mbps). The $0.196/Mbps figure is cheapest plan price ÷ FCC's fleet-wide median speed, not an apples-to-apples plan-to-speed match — both columns are kept visible in the table above rather than resolved silently.
  • DSL is degenerate in 2 states. FCC's reported median download speed for DSL rounds to 0 Mbps in NJ (65,253 locations) and VA (271,861 locations) — almost certainly legacy copper barely providing real service, not a data error. Cost-per-Mbps is mathematically undefined (division by zero) in both and shown as such in the table rather than smoothed over.
  • Fixed Wireless is three technologies, not one. FCC splits it into Unlicensed (code 70), Licensed (71), and Licensed-by-Rule/LBR (72). An earlier version of this page collapsed all three into one "Fixed Wireless" row and let 71/72 fall into an unlabeled "Other" bucket — which mattered, since Licensed Fixed Wireless (the actual technology behind Verizon 5G Home / T-Mobile Home Internet) turned out to be the single largest fixed-wireless population by far in every state. Now split and priced correctly; LBR has no matching published pricing in any state, so it's shown in the table but excluded from the range chart and left unpriced rather than researched for a niche product.
  • Pricing is a snapshot, not a full market. One or two representative plans per technology (cheapest published tier), not an exhaustive rate survey; national averages fill gaps where a specific regional provider wasn't researched.
  • Choropleth map now plots all 50 states + DC (v3.4), not just the contiguous 48 + DC. Alaska and Hawaii were previously excluded from the map's rendering (though not from any table or the underlying data) over antimeridian-wrap and disconnected-islands concerns. The map already used d3's AlbersUSA projection, which solves exactly that problem by repositioning AK/HI as bottom-left insets — so the exclusion was unnecessary. Alaska turns out to be the map's actual most subsidy-intense state ($784/location), which the map's legend previously couldn't show since AK wasn't plotted.

Personal reliability layer — in progress

A lightweight logger (Cloudflare speed-test endpoints + ping, no external dependencies) is running every 30 minutes on two machines, each on its own home broadband connection, logging download/upload throughput, latency, jitter, and packet loss to a local database. The logger also supports labeling a session as a tethered/cellular connection (--label cellular-tether), but no cellular data has actually been collected yet — both machines are on home Wi-Fi so far. Satellite is intentionally out of scope for this layer for now (no satellite hardware access); it stays in the market-layer analysis above. This needs a few weeks of runway before the personal-vs-market overlay is meaningful — check back for an update once there's enough data to say something real.
